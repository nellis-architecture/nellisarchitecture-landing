Choosing an interior designer often begins with an image.

You see a beautiful room on Instagram, a completed home in a magazine or a collection of interiors on a studio's website. Something about the materials, colours or atmosphere catches your attention, and you begin imagining what that designer might create for you.

Search for the top interior design companies in Dubai and most of what comes up is the same kind of ranked list, useful for a first impression but a poor way to judge whether a firm is right for your home.

There is nothing wrong with starting there. Design is visual, and it is important to feel a connection with the work a studio produces.

## **But photographs can only tell you so much.**

A beautifully styled room does not necessarily reveal how well it functions, how carefully it was detailed or how successfully the design was carried through construction. And with [digital visualisation and artificial intelligence](https://nellisarchitecture.com/news/ai-in-home-design-useful-or-hype/) making it increasingly easy to produce convincing images, the distinction between an attractive concept and a completed project is becoming more important.

When choosing an interior design firm, the question should not only be whether you like what you see. It should be whether the team behind it can understand what you need and deliver it.

## **Looking beyond the images**

One of the first things I would encourage anyone to look for is evidence of completed work.

A portfolio should give you a sense of a studio's design language, but it should also help you understand the range and quality of projects the team has actually delivered.

There is a significant difference between developing a compelling concept and seeing that concept through to completion.

In a finished space, every design decision has been tested against real conditions. Materials have been installed, details have been resolved, furniture has been fitted and the various elements have had to work together.

Where possible, it is valuable to look at completed projects, speak to previous clients or understand the experience of the people who will be responsible for your project.

Recommendations and word of mouth remain important for exactly this reason. They offer insight into things that photographs cannot show, such as how a team communicates, manages challenges and responds when something does not go according to plan.

## **Of course, you should still feel a connection with the studio's work.**

You might not like every detail of every project, but you should recognise an approach or design sensibility that resonates with you.

That connection is a starting point. It should not be the only reason you make the decision.

### **Start with specialism, not size**

Before judging anyone's portfolio, it is worth working out what you are comparing. A firm known for hotel interiors is not automatically the right fit for a private villa, and the reverse is just as true.

In Dubai, most interior design work falls into one of three broad categories: [residential and villa interiors](https://nellisarchitecture.com/studio/interior-design/), commercial fit-outs and hospitality or F&B interiors. Each tends to differ in material selection, project pace and how far brand guidelines shape the design versus one client's own taste. A firm whose recent work leans toward hospitality or retail may do excellent work, but that is a different skill set to a private villa, where the brief comes from one family's way of living rather than a brand standard.

### **The importance of understanding the client**

A successful interior design project depends on communication.

That sounds obvious, but it becomes more complicated when you consider how many people may be involved in a large residential project.

Sometimes we work directly with the homeowner. On other projects, particularly larger luxury villas, communication happens through client representatives, project managers or other members of the client's team.

Each additional person creates another opportunity for information to be interpreted differently.

We have experienced situations where a reference project was provided as an example of what a client liked, only to discover later that they wanted something quite different for their new home.

The reference itself was not necessarily wrong. The problem was that its intended meaning had not been fully understood.

That is why [a clear design brief](https://nellisarchitecture.com/news/25-questions-the-art-of-a-good-brief/) is so important. At Nellis Architecture, we use a structured process to understand the client's preferences, requirements and expectations before moving too far into the design.

We want to know more than which colours or materials someone likes.

How will the spaces be used? Who will live there? What does a normal day look like? How do they entertain? Which existing pieces of furniture or artwork are important to them? What do they want to feel when they enter a particular room?

These conversations help establish the direction of the project, but they also create a shared understanding that can guide decisions later.

A good interior designer should be able to listen carefully, interpret what a client is saying and sometimes ask questions the client has not yet considered.

The relationship should feel collaborative rather than transactional.

## **What happens behind the finished room**

One of the biggest misconceptions about interior design is that it is primarily about selecting furniture, fabrics, colours and decorative finishes.

Those choices are important, but they represent only part of the work.

My own training was in interior architecture, which approaches a building from the inside out.

Where an architect may begin by considering the overall building, its form and its relationship with the site, interior architecture looks closely at how internal spaces are constructed, experienced and used.

That requires a detailed understanding of how buildings work.

Consider something as simple as a headboard.

It might appear in a rendering as a beautifully finished feature behind a bed, but the actual design can involve the construction of the wall, the supporting framework, the fixing methods, integrated lighting, electrical coordination, material junctions and the way every visible edge is finished.

The same is true of joinery, wall cladding, shelving, ceiling features and built-in furniture.

It is not enough to decide where something should go. We need to understand how it will be made, how it will be fixed in place and how all the different elements will come together.

That work is often invisible in the finished photograph. And in many ways, that is the point.

When detailing is done well, the space should feel resolved. You should not be distracted by awkward junctions, exposed fixings or elements that appear disconnected from one another.

Achieving that apparent simplicity can require an extraordinary amount of thought.

## **Creating a home rather than a collection of rooms**

Another challenge that people sometimes underestimate is the importance of consistency across an entire project.

It is relatively easy to find a beautiful chair, an interesting fabric or a striking material.

The more difficult task is understanding how those individual choices contribute to the home as a whole.

In a large villa, there may be numerous bedrooms, living areas, entertainment spaces, bathrooms and private rooms, each with its own purpose and character.

They should not all feel identical. But neither should they feel as though they were designed independently of one another.

Materials, proportions, colours, lighting and architectural details need to establish relationships between spaces.

Sometimes that means repeating a particular material in different ways. Sometimes it means creating contrast while maintaining a consistent underlying design language.

The objective is not simply to make each room attractive. It is to create an interior that feels coherent as you move through the building.

This is where interior architecture and interior decoration become distinct, although they often work closely together.

Decoration can introduce furniture, textiles, artwork and the finishing elements that bring personality into a space.

Interior architecture considers the space itself, including its planning, construction, material relationships and detailed integration with the building.

For a successful project, those disciplines need to work together.

## **Choosing the right partnership**

There is no single checklist that can guarantee a successful relationship with an interior design firm.

Every client is different, and every project brings its own requirements.

But there are a few questions I believe are worth asking before making a decision.

Does the studio's work resonate with you? Can they demonstrate relevant experience and explain how their projects move from concept to completion? Do they have a clear process for understanding your requirements? And, perhaps most importantly, do you feel comfortable communicating with the people who will be designing your space?

A good designer should bring expertise, but they should also be prepared to listen.

They should be able to explain their decisions, challenge an idea when necessary and understand that the finished project needs to reflect the client rather than simply the designer's personal preferences.

At Nellis Architecture, that is central to how we approach interiors.

Because we design both the architecture and the interiors ourselves, we consider them together from day one instead of fitting one around the other after the fact. That is often the biggest practical difference between a firm that does both and one that only does interiors.

We are interested in beautiful materials, considered detailing and spaces that feel carefully composed. But those things only become meaningful when they support the way people actually live.

A home is not a portfolio image. It is somewhere people will spend years of their lives.

They will move through its rooms every day, gather with family, entertain friends, work, rest and gradually make the spaces their own.

**Choosing the right interior design firm is therefore not simply about finding someone who can create the look you have in mind.** It is about finding a team that can understand the life behind that image and design a space that works just as well as it looks.

## **What do interior design companies in Dubai charge?**

Pricing depends on project scope and specification level: a full villa and a single room sit at opposite ends of that range, and standard joinery costs differently to fully bespoke work. A good firm should be able to walk you through how their fee structure works rather than quote a flat number upfront.

## **How long does a full villa interior design project take?**

Timelines depend on scope, and on whether construction or renovation work is involved alongside the interior design itself, so it is worth asking any firm you are considering for a realistic timeline based on your specific project rather than a general industry figure.
