The neighbouring villa with a clear line into the pool deck. The boardwalk that runs closer than the render suggested. The boat passing at the exact moment the family is having breakfast on the terrace.

 

Clients rarely arrive asking for privacy or view. They ask for both, on the reasonable assumption that architecture will find a way to deliver them together. It can. But only when the two are resolved as one problem from the earliest sketch, rather than treated as a design decision followed by a damage-control one, where curtains, frosting or a second wall are added once the glazing has already been drawn.

 

This is the quiet discipline increasingly expected of waterfront homes in Dubai: they feel enclosed without feeling walled in, and open without feeling exposed.

 

As Lee Nellis often puts it, a view you cannot enjoy without feeling watched is not really a view. It is a liability with good lighting.

 

### The False Choice Between Glass and Wall

 

Most waterfront briefs default to one of two instincts. The first is to maximise glazing, treating floor-to-ceiling glass as the point of the entire project. The second is to compensate for the exposure that creates by adding height, screens or planting after the fact, often undoing the openness the glazing was meant to provide.

 

Both instincts share the same mistake. They treat privacy and view as a trade-off to be balanced, rather than a geometry problem to be solved. Sightlines are specific. A person standing in a neighbouring first-floor bedroom can see into a ground-floor lounge at one angle and nothing at all a metre to the left.

 

A boardwalk sightline drops away entirely once a terrace is set half a level below grade. These are measurable conditions, not aesthetic preferences, and they can be designed around with precision rather than guessed at with glass tint.

 

Once privacy is understood as a question of angle, distance and elevation rather than a question of how much glass to allow, the apparent conflict with the view mostly disappears. What remains is a design problem, and design problems can be solved well.

 

### Reading the Plot Before Reading the Brief

 

On a waterfront plot, the client brief is only half the information required. The other half comes from the site itself, and it needs to be read before a single room is placed on the plan.

 

This means mapping exactly who can see into the plot, from where, and at what time of day. Which windows in the neighbouring villa face this frontage. How close the public walkway sits to the boundary. Whether the beach or marina view is genuinely private or shared with passing traffic on the water. How the sun moves across the site, and which hours of the day bring the strongest glare off the water back into the home.

 

For oceanfront communities such as Palm Jumeirah, where plots sit close together and views are often shared across several properties, this mapping is not a preliminary exercise. It is the foundation the entire design rests on. A home designed without this understanding may capture a spectacular view in the render and still feel exposed the first week the family moves in.

 

### Massing Does the First Work

 

Long before glazing, shading or landscape are considered, the massing of the building is already deciding how private the home will feel. These decisions do more to resolve privacy than any material specified afterwards: how volumes are arranged, where upper floors step back, which elevation is allowed to open fully and which is deliberately more restrained.

 

In Villa Aurea on Palm Jumeirah, this played out directly in how the building was oriented on its plot. The social and living spaces were positioned to open fully toward the water, while the massing along the neighbouring boundary was handled with more restraint, using level changes and considered window placement rather than glazing at the same scale as the sea-facing elevation.

 

The result is a home that reads as open and generous from the water, and considered and private from the side that matters just as much day to day.

 

This is the advantage of resolving massing early. The building does the work of separating public and private sightlines before any screen, shutter or curtain is asked to do it after the fact.

 

### Privacy That Isn’t a Wall

 

The more effective approach uses landscape, level and screening in combination. Layered planting positioned at the specific height and angle a sightline requires, rather than a continuous hedge along the boundary. Timber or stone screening with a defined sightline angle, open at the angles that matter and closed at the ones that don’t.

 

This is a principle we have applied inland as much as on the coast. In Miro, Umm Al Sheif, villas sit close together on tightly configured plots, where privacy was resolved through setback, planting and section, rather than perimeter walling. The same approach translates to the water, where the cost of a solid boundary is a lost view, not a lost garden.

 

A garden designed with privacy in mind does not announce itself as a privacy measure. It simply reads as landscape, while quietly doing the work of a wall without ever looking like one.

 

### Section Does What Plan Cannot

 

As Quinton Murdoch often notes, privacy is rarely a plan problem. It is a section problem that people keep trying to solve on the wrong drawing.

 

A sunken lounge set half a level below the surrounding terrace removes it from a neighbour’s sightline without a single additional wall. An elevated primary suite placed above the sightline of an adjacent property gains privacy and a better view in the same move.

 

A double-height living space angled toward the water, with a lower, more enclosed volume behind it facing the boundary, allows one elevation to be generous and the other to be quiet. These are structural and spatial decisions, made at the same stage the massing is resolved, not adjustments made once the drawings are complete.

 

### The Discipline of Automated Shading

 

Motorised louvres that respond to sun angle rather than a fixed timer. Glazing that shifts opacity in response to occupancy rather than requiring a switch to be found and pressed. Positioned and specified correctly, these systems resolve the handful of conditions that architecture alone cannot fully anticipate, without ever becoming the primary privacy strategy.

 

Smart home automation, relied on by clients across Dubai for this purpose, works best as the final adjustment to a home already designed to be private, not as the correction for one that wasn’t.

 

### Designing for the Neighbours, Not Just the Street

 

![Designing for the Neighbours, Not Just the Street](https://nellisarchitecture.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Zoya6-1024x576.jpg)

 

On tightly configured waterfront communities, particularly across the fronds of Palm Jumeirah, the more consequential relationship is often not between the home and the public street, but between the home and the villa next door. Plots sit close enough that a poorly considered window or an unshaded upper-floor terrace can compromise both properties at once.

 

This means a genuinely private waterfront home is not designed in isolation. It requires understanding the neighbouring plot’s massing, its window positions and its likely future development, not only the client’s own brief.

 

Architecture firms in Dubai that treat each plot as a closed system, without reference to what sits either side of it, tend to produce homes that perform well in isolation and less well in practice, once the neighbouring property is occupied.

 

### What This Means for Clients Building on the Water

 

As Matas Belevicius often reflects, the best waterfront homes don’t ask you to choose between feeling protected and feeling connected to the water. If you’re being asked to choose, the design hasn’t gone far enough yet.

 

For clients evaluating luxury home design Dubai firms for a waterfront project, the most useful question is not how much glass a practice can specify, or how sophisticated its automated shading is. It is how early privacy is considered relative to the view, and whether the two are being designed as one problem or handled as two separate ones.

 

### Frequently Asked Questions

 

Can a Palm Jumeirah villa be private without losing its sea view?

 

Yes, and the two are usually resolved together rather than traded against each other. Privacy on a Palm Jumeirah plot is achieved primarily through orientation, massing and level changes decided early in the design, with landscape and automated shading used to manage the specific angles and times of day that architecture alone cannot fully control. Solid boundary walls are rarely the right answer, since they remove the view along with the sightline they were meant to block.

 

What is the best way to add privacy to a waterfront home without building higher walls?

 

Layered planting, level changes such as sunken terraces or elevated suites, and screening positioned at the specific angle a sightline requires all work more effectively than a taller wall, and without sacrificing the view. A wall solves a sightline problem in one direction only. Landscape and section can be tuned to the exact angle that needs privacy while leaving everything else open.

 

Does automated or smart glass actually improve privacy in a luxury villa?

 

It helps, but it works best as a final layer rather than the main strategy. Switchable glazing and automated shading are effective for managing specific, changing conditions – low sun, an occupied neighbouring terrace, a particular time of day – once the home’s massing, orientation and landscape have already resolved the majority of the privacy requirement. Relying on smart glass alone, without those earlier decisions, tends to produce a home that needs constant management rather than one that simply feels private.

 

### Design Intent Summary

 

This article positions Nellis Architecture as a practice that resolves privacy and view as a single design problem rather than a compromise, using orientation, massing, section and landscape ahead of glazing and technology. It draws on Villa Aurea and Miro as evidence of this approach applied across two distinct contexts – open waterfront and tightly configured inland plots – and speaks directly to clients commissioning oceanfront villas who want an uninterrupted sea view without sacrificing the sense of seclusion a private residence should provide.
