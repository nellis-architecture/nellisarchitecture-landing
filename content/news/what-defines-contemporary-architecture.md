**By Matas Belevicius**

When someone asks for contemporary architecture, there is often a fairly immediate image that comes to mind.

**Large expanses of glass, clean lines, flat roofs, simple forms and buildings that look unmistakably new.**

There is nothing necessarily wrong with any of those things. But somewhere along the way, we have started using the word contemporary to describe a particular appearance rather than a way of thinking about architecture.

**I find that interesting because architecture has never really stood still.**

Many of the buildings we now consider historic were, at the time they were built, introducing new ideas about materials, construction, space and the way people lived. What seemed radical in one generation became familiar in another.

Even modern architecture, which we often use as another way of describing something contemporary, refers to a particular movement in architectural history. Architects such as Mies van der Rohe and Le Corbusier were already exploring those ideas more than a century ago.

So perhaps the more useful question is not what contemporary architecture looks like. *It is what it means to [practise architecture in the present](https://nellisarchitecture.com/studio/architecture-design/).*

That is the question this piece answers: what sets a contemporary architect's approach apart.

For me, that means learning from what came before us, understanding the possibilities available today and designing buildings that will continue to make sense in the future.

It sounds simple, but it changes the way you approach a project.

## **What contemporary architects can learn from what lasts**

We talk a great deal about [timeless architecture](https://nellisarchitecture.com/news/the-craft-of-time-why-enduring-architecture-matters/).

It is a phrase that appears in almost every conversation about design, but I am not sure we always stop to consider what it actually means.

A building does not become timeless simply because it has a restrained colour palette or avoids decorative elements. Nor does it need to follow a particular architectural style.

Some of the most enduring buildings in the world are extraordinarily detailed. Others are remarkably simple.

What they often share is an underlying understanding of proportion, composition, material and context.

Those principles have developed over centuries, and they remain relevant regardless of the technology or architectural language available at a particular moment.

You can look at a building from hundreds of years ago and recognise that its proportions feel right, even if you know very little about architecture.

There is a relationship between the parts of the building that makes sense.

The same applies to materials. Natural stone, timber and metal have been used across generations because we understand their qualities and how they behave over time. That does not mean newer materials should be avoided, but it does mean we should be thoughtful about what we choose and why.

One of the things I believe strongly is that simplicity should not be confused with minimalism.

A building can be rich in character and detail while still having clarity in its design.

The question is whether each element contributes something meaningful or whether we are adding complexity simply because we can.

Perhaps that is one of the most important lessons contemporary architecture can take from the past.

Good architecture does not necessarily need more. It needs the right things.

## **The problem with pretending in contemporary architecture**

There is another aspect of contemporary architecture that I think deserves more attention: honesty.

Architecture tends to age badly when it is trying too hard to be something it is not.

We see that when buildings imitate historical styles without understanding the conditions that originally produced them.

A historic palace, for example, is the result of a particular period, culture, construction process and often generations of craftsmanship. Reproducing its appearance today does not necessarily reproduce the qualities that made it significant.

There is a difference between [learning from history and copying its image](https://nellisarchitecture.com/news/heritage-and-innovation-in-dubai-architecture-how-tradition-meets-modern-design/).

We have written about that distinction already, in [Modern vs Contemporary Villas on the Palm](https://nellisarchitecture.com/news/modern-vs-contemporary-villas-on-the-palm/). This piece stays with the mindset behind it, not the building's style.

The same problem appears in a very different form when we look at [sustainability](https://nellisarchitecture.com/news/sustainable-architecture-for-dubai-homes/).

A building can have greenery covering its façade and still perform poorly in environmental terms.

Adding planters does not automatically make architecture sustainable.

The more important questions are about how the building works. How does it respond to the sun? How much energy does it require? How does it conserve that energy? Are the materials appropriate for the climate and the purpose of the building?

These are less visible decisions, but they are often far more important than the image being presented.

For me, that is where architectural honesty becomes relevant.

If a material is used, there should be a reason for it. If a building claims to respond to its environment, that response should be evident in the way it performs.

And if we are borrowing from another architectural tradition, we should understand what made it meaningful in the first place.

Architecture should not have to pretend in order to be interesting.

## **Designing for a future we cannot predict**

One of the challenges of architecture is that we are always designing for a future we cannot fully understand.

A home being designed today may [still be occupied twenty or thirty years from now](https://nellisarchitecture.com/news/future-proofing-a-palm-jumeirah-home-luxury-villa-design-dubai-nellis-architecture/).

By then, the family living in it may have changed considerably. Children grow up, lifestyles evolve, technology develops and the way spaces are used can become very different from what was originally imagined.

That makes adaptability an important part of good design.

We should be thinking about how a building can accommodate change rather than designing every space so rigidly that it only works for one particular moment in time.

The same applies to the way a building relates to its surroundings.

Before considering a visual style, we need to understand the location, climate, culture and people who will use it.

[A house in Dubai](https://nellisarchitecture.com/news/how-dubais-climate-shapes-every-decision-a-luxury-architect-makes/) should not automatically look or function like a house in England. The conditions are different, and the architecture should respond accordingly.

Mashrabiya screens are a good example: they get reinterpreted for contemporary Dubai villas rather than copied directly, as covered in [Privacy in Luxury Villa Architecture: From Mashrabiya to Modern Screens](https://nellisarchitecture.com/news/privacy-in-luxury-villa-architecture-dubai-mashrabiya-to-modern-screens/).

This is something we consider across our projects at [Nellis Architecture](https://nellisarchitecture.com/).

We work in [different countries and environments](https://nellisarchitecture.com/projects/), but the intention is never to repeat the same building in a different location.

The consistency should come from the principles behind the design.

Understanding the context, creating balanced proportions, selecting materials carefully and designing around how people live are more important than making every project share the same appearance.

A building that responds to those things has a better chance of remaining relevant than one designed primarily around a passing trend.

## **Using new tools without losing the architect**

Of course, being a contemporary architect also means embracing the possibilities of the present.

The tools available to architects have changed enormously.

Building information modelling, computational design and parametric modelling have already transformed how we develop, test and coordinate ideas.

[Artificial intelligence](https://nellisarchitecture.com/news/ai-in-home-design-useful-or-hype/) is now introducing another set of possibilities.

What interests me is not simply that technology allows us to do things faster. It is how creatively we can use it to explore ideas, understand problems and make better decisions.

But I do not believe that having access to better tools automatically produces better architecture.

You can have the most advanced software available and still design a building that does not respond to its site or the people who will use it.

Technology needs judgement. It needs curiosity. And, perhaps most importantly, it needs creativity.

The architect still has to decide which questions are worth asking, which possibilities are worth exploring and when a particular design decision is appropriate.

That responsibility does not disappear because the tools become more sophisticated. In many ways, it becomes more important.

The opportunity is to combine the knowledge architecture has accumulated over centuries with new ways of thinking and working. Not to abandon one for the other.

## **What makes architecture contemporary?**

When I think about the architects whose work has influenced the profession, it is difficult to single out one person.

From Mies van der Rohe to Tadao Ando and Norman Foster, different generations have brought different ideas about what architecture could become.

What makes their work worth studying is not simply the appearance of the buildings. It is the thinking behind them.

They worked with the materials, technologies and possibilities of their time while continuing to engage with fundamental architectural questions. That is something we should continue doing.

There will always be new materials, new technologies and new design trends. Buildings that look contemporary today will eventually become part of architectural history.

Some will age well. Others may begin to feel dated surprisingly quickly.

I suspect the difference will have less to do with whether they followed the visual language of their time and more to do with whether the underlying architecture was considered carefully.

* Did the building understand its surroundings?
* Was it designed around the people who would use it?
* Were its materials chosen with purpose?
* Could it accommodate change?
* Did it balance beauty with function?

These are questions architects have been asking for generations. And they are still the questions that matter.

*Perhaps contemporary architecture is not about creating buildings that look as though they belong to the future.* It is about using everything we know today to create buildings that will still make sense when the future arrives.

## **What is the difference between modern and contemporary architecture?**

Modern architecture names a specific design movement from the twentieth century, built on a fairly fixed set of principles. Contemporary architecture has no single style: it covers whatever is being designed and built now, so it can draw on many different influences and eras at once, as covered in [Modern vs Contemporary Villas on the Palm](https://nellisarchitecture.com/news/modern-vs-contemporary-villas-on-the-palm/).
