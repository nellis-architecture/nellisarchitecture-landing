**By Lee Nellis and Fiona Webb-Johnston**

The final 15 percent of a project is often the part that feels the longest.

By that stage, most of the big things are already there. The walls are up, the glass is in, the major services are working and, from the outside, it can look almost finished.

But anyone who has built a house, fitted out an office or [worked through the final stages of a project](https://nellisarchitecture.com/news/aurea-on-site-protecting-the-design-as-it-becomes-real/) will know that "almost finished" can contain an extraordinary amount of work.

Our [new office at Expo City Dubai](https://nellisarchitecture.com/contact/) has been a useful reminder of that.

It has also taught us more about workplace design than any brief or precedent study could have.

Over the past few weeks, the space has started changing quickly. Furniture has arrived, lighting and sound systems are being programmed, appliances are being installed and the protective coverings are finally coming off the joinery, worktops and finishes we have been looking at for months.

That is the point where a project begins to feel different.

Earlier in construction, progress is obvious. A wall appears. A ceiling closes. A large piece of glass is installed. These are major interventions and each one transforms the space.

Towards the end, the work becomes smaller and more detailed. A missing fascia panel. A lighting scene that still needs adjusting. A damaged surface that has to be remade. A bookshelf that is complete but has been hidden behind plastic for weeks.

Individually, none of these things feels particularly significant.

Together, they are the difference between a building that is nearly finished and a space that actually feels complete.

## **When the space starts to reveal itself**

One of the most satisfying parts of this stage is seeing materials at their real scale for the first time.

Throughout the design process, we work with samples, drawings, [renders and mock-ups](https://nellisarchitecture.com/news/seeing-beyond-the-surface-my-journey-in-architectural-visualisation/). They allow us to understand colour, texture, proportion and intent, but they can never completely recreate what happens when everything comes together in the actual space.

A small material sample might tell you what a finish looks like, but not what it will feel like across an entire wall. A lighting sample may give you an idea of the effect, but not what happens when that feature is suspended across a large room. A render can show you the design, but it cannot fully replicate daylight moving through the space, the way different materials sit next to each other or how the room will feel once people are inside it.

There is always a point where you have to trust the design.

The conference room has probably become the best example of that for us.

The table itself has had more than its fair share of challenges. The Dekton surface arrived in multiple panels and one was damaged in transit. The 3D-printed base was also damaged and had to be remade. The lighting feature above it was another element we could only really understand once it was installed at full scale.

Now, as those pieces finally come together, the room is starting to feel like the space we imagined.

In a few months, clients will sit around that table without knowing how many drawings, conversations, deliveries, remakes and decisions went into it. That is probably how it should be.

Good spaces should feel effortless once they are complete. The effort belongs in the process.

## **Designing the workplace around how people actually work**

What has been more interesting, though, is seeing how the office is beginning to reveal the way we hope people will use it.

For a long time, offices were largely designed around numbers. How many people are there? How many desks do we need? How many meeting rooms can we fit into the floorplate?

Those things still matter, but they are not really the starting point.

The more important question is how people actually work.

This is what [human-centred design](https://nellisarchitecture.com/news/luxury-villa-design-psychology-dubai/) means in an office. You look at how people behave first, then work out the desks and ratios around that.

Some parts of the day require concentration. Others require conversation. Sometimes you need a quiet place for a call. Sometimes you need to spread drawings across a table and pull three people into the discussion. Sometimes you simply need to leave your desk for half an hour and work somewhere that feels different.

That has shaped a number of the spaces in our office. The library and material area, for example, was never intended to be a traditional library. It has a large table where drawings can be laid out, materials can be handled and compared, and someone can work away from their usual desk if they need a change of environment.

Now that the shelves are being uncovered and the books, samples and objects are beginning to go in, the room is starting to feel completely different.

It has colour and texture. More importantly, it has a purpose that extends beyond its name.

The kitchen is another example. On a drawing, a kitchen is simply another functional space. In reality, it may become one of the most important parts of the office.

It is where people make coffee, stop between meetings, talk about something they are working on or have a conversation they probably would not have scheduled formally.

Those spaces between the obvious workspaces matter more than we sometimes realise.

An office is not only a collection of desks and meeting rooms. The informal moments between them are part of how a team works too.

## **Making the office worth coming to**

That question has become more relevant as the way people work has changed.

People are used to having more flexibility now. They know what it feels like to work from home, to take a call somewhere quiet or to structure parts of their day differently.

That means the office cannot simply assume people will want to be there.

It has to offer something that working elsewhere cannot.

That is a retention question as much as a design one. A team that feels better at work is more likely to stay.

For us, that is not about gimmicks. It is about creating a workplace that gives people choice.

A place where you can concentrate when you need to. Somewhere you can collaborate when the work calls for it. Somewhere comfortable enough to spend the day, with natural light, good acoustics, appropriate materials and spaces that do not all ask you to work in exactly the same way.

Comfort is often one of those things you notice most when it is missing.

During construction, many of the windows were covered. There was scaffolding, dust, temporary lighting and ongoing work throughout the space.

As those coverings have come down, one of the biggest transformations has simply been daylight returning to the rooms.

Suddenly the materials look different. The rooms feel larger. You begin to understand what it will actually be like to spend a day there.

That is an important part of workplace design for us. [Wellness should not be something applied at the end](https://nellisarchitecture.com/news/crafting-wellness-at-nellis-architecture-a-year-of-reflection-and-growth/) through a few plants and some decorative language. It begins with basic questions about how people feel in a space.

Is there enough natural light? Can someone move somewhere quieter when they need to focus? Does the space feel comfortable? Can people choose a different environment during the day rather than remaining in one position from morning to evening?

Those decisions affect the experience of the office far more than any single feature.

## **Workplace design starts with the same questions we ask at home**

In many ways, designing our own office has reinforced something we already understand through [our residential work](https://nellisarchitecture.com/projects/).

[When we design a home](https://nellisarchitecture.com/studio/architecture-design/), we do not begin with a list of rooms and arrange them into a plan.

We begin with the people.

We want to understand how the family lives, where they naturally gather, how they entertain, where they want privacy and how they move through the house during an ordinary day.

The architecture grows from those behaviours.

A workplace should not be approached differently.

The brief is not simply twenty desks, a kitchen, a boardroom and a reception.

[The real brief](https://nellisarchitecture.com/news/25-questions-the-art-of-a-good-brief/) is the people using them.

Where do they need concentration? Where do they need to come together? Where will spontaneous conversations happen? What will make someone comfortable enough to spend the day there?

The typology changes, but the principle is the same.

Design starts with understanding how people live and work.

It is the same thinking behind [Beyond Aesthetics: Designing Spaces That Last](https://nellisarchitecture.com/news/beyond-aesthetics-designing-spaces-that-last/), where design starts from how people live, rather than how a space looks in a photo.

## **When does an office become finished?**

We originally thought there would be a particular moment when the office suddenly felt complete.

Perhaps it would be when the signage went up. It was not.

The signage arrived and almost immediately our attention moved to everything that still needed to happen.

Perhaps it will be when the last approval comes through, or the final piece of furniture is installed. We do not think so anymore.

The real moment will probably be much more ordinary.

It will be the first morning when the team arrives and everyone begins working.

Until then, this is still a project. Once people start using it, it becomes an office.

That distinction is important because, as architects, we spend months imagining spaces before anyone inhabits them. We predict movement, behaviour and interaction. We decide where people may want privacy, where they might gather and where they may stop for an unplanned conversation.

But [architecture is only really tested once life enters it](https://nellisarchitecture.com/news/when-design-is-tested-what-actually-matters/).

People may use a space exactly as we imagined.

They may also surprise us.

A corner we thought was incidental may become someone's favourite place to work. The library may be used more than the meeting rooms. The kitchen table may become the place where the best conversations happen. That is the part we are looking forward to most.

When we first took possession of the space at Expo City Dubai, it was essentially a shell. A few months later, it has transformed completely.

There are still things to finish, approvals to secure and final layers to add. Even after we move in, there will still be plants, books, objects and other pieces that gradually find their place.

Perhaps that is the real lesson of the final 15 percent. Construction finishes first. Then the human layer begins.

People arrive, books fill shelves, drawings spread across tables, coffee gets made, meetings happen and spaces start being used in ways you could never completely predict on a drawing.

That is when architecture stops being something we designed. And starts becoming somewhere people actually want to be.
